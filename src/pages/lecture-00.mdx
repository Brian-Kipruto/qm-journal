This lecture starts by drawing a parallel between light and electrons. For light waves, the intensity (which tells you the number of photons in a given volume) is proportional to the amplitude of the electric field squared, $E^2$.

So an electron is a quantum particle — it's both a particle and a wave. Because of this double behaviour, we describe them using a mathematical tool known as the **wave function**, represented by the greek letter psi ($\psi$).

But what is the physical variable that is actually *waving* in an electron wave? It is not a physical wave like a ripple in water — instead it's a **probability amplitude**. In order to find the actual probability of locating the electron in a specific tiny spot (like a small volume element $dV$ or a small 1-d length $dx$), you take the absolute square of that wave function, $|\psi|^2$. This squared value is known as the **probability density**.

In a nutshell, the wave function itself ($\psi$) is just a *complex-valued mathematical description of a quantum particle that acts as a probability amplitude*, but when you square it ($|\psi|^2$) it tells you exactly where to find the particle in a specific location.

### The two plots, side by side

The wave function ψ oscillates above and below the center line — it has both positive and negative values. Because it can be negative, ψ itself does not represent a physical quantity like count or amount. It represents probability amplitude.

The probability density $|\psi|^2$ is what you get when you square it. All values are now positive — squaring the negative parts of ψ makes them positive. This is the graph that tells you the probability of finding the particle at a specific location. **The higher the peak, the more likely you are to find the particle there.** The valleys that touch zero are places you will *never* find the particle.

The interference pattern from a double-slit experiment is the real-world visualization of this. Bright bands correspond to the peaks in $|\psi|^2$ — constructive interference, high probability of finding particles. Dark bands correspond to zeros — destructive interference, near-zero probability.

So, how do wave functions actually work?

Because electron waves can be described using mathematical equations that include imaginary numbers (using $i$), $\psi$ itself is always a **complex** function.

<Aside title="Imaginary numbers">

Imaginary numbers, simply put, are numbers that give a negative result when you square them. In standard math, squaring a number — whether positive or negative — always gives you a positive result. To solve problems where you need the square root of a negative number, mathematicians created the unit $i$.

**Definition:** $i = \sqrt{-1}$ 
**The rule:** $i^2 = -1$

Think of them as not "fake" but an *extra dimension*. While real numbers live on the left-to-right line, imaginary numbers live on a vertical line. Together, they create **complex numbers**, which engineers and scientists use to describe things that rotate or wave — like electricity and sound.

A summarry of the cycle would be sth like
- **Forward**:  start with -5 $\rightarrow$ squareroot it $\rightarrow$  $\sqrt{-5}$ = $^i\sqrt{5}$
- **Inverse**: $^i\sqrt{5}$ $\rightarrow$ square it $\rightarrow$ $(^i\sqrt{5})^2 = -5$
</Aside>

However, in the real world, you cannot have an *imaginary* probability of finding an electron. The probability has to be a real, measurable function.

In order to turn that complex mathematical wave into a real-world probability density $|\psi|^2$, we use a tool called the **complex conjugate** ($\psi^*$).

- To find a complex conjugate, you simply take your wave function and change every $i$ to $-i$.
- When you multiply the original wave function by its complex conjugate ($\psi^* \cdot \psi$), the imaginary parts completely cancel out mathematically. You're left with $a^2 + b^2$, which is always a real number.

<Section letter="A" title="Rules of a valid wave function" />

Because the resulting number (probability density) represents a real physical quantity — the probability of finding the particle — a valid wave function is bound by some strict real-world constraints:

1. **$\psi(x)$ must be finite, continuous, and single-valued.** You cannot have a broken wave, an infinite wave, or a wave that gives two different probabilities for the exact same spot. The probability has to be perfectly well defined everywhere.

2. **The derivative $d\psi/dx$ must also be finite, continuous, and single-valued.** In QM, the slope (derivative) of the wave is directly tied to the particle's classical momentum, so it has to be a smooth continuous value.

3. **The sign does not matter.** Whether the wave dips into negative numbers has no physical significance, because we always take its absolute square to find the probability. The negative sign disappears.

<Example n={3} title="The free particle">
Consider the wave function for a free particle, $\psi(x) = Ae^{ikx}$. At what value of $x$ is the particle most likely to be found at a given time?

This is basically asking for the probability density, and to find that we multiply the wave function by its complex conjugate (simply turning the imaginary numbers into negatives):

$$|\psi(x)|^2 = \psi^*(x) \cdot \psi(x)$$

$$\psi(x) = Ae^{ikx}$$
$$\psi^*(x) = Ae^{-ikx}$$
$$|\psi(x)|^2 = (Ae^{-ikx})(Ae^{ikx})$$

Since $e^{-ikx} \cdot e^{ikx} = e^0 = 1$, we have:

$$|\psi(x)|^2 = |A|^2$$

The probability density is a **constant value $|A|^2$** — meaning the particle is equally likely to be found at any value of $x$. There is no specific most likely position.

In physics terms, we say the particle is completely **delocalized**. Because we know its momentum exactly, the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle tells us we have maximum uncertainty about its position.
</Example>

<Example n={4} title="A free electron, numerically">
A free electron has wave function $\psi(x) = Ae^{i(2.4 \times 10^{11})x}$, where $x$ is in meters. Find:

**1. Its de Broglie wavelength.**

The wave number $k = 2.4 \times 10^{11}$ m⁻¹. It's related to the de Broglie wavelength by $k = 2\pi / \lambda$, so:

$$\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = \frac{2\pi}{2.4 \times 10^{11}} \approx 2.62 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m}$$

**2. Its momentum.**

Momentum is related to wave number by $p = \hbar k$:

$$p = (1.054 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J·s}) \times (2.4 \times 10^{11} \text{ m}^{-1}) \approx 2.53 \times 10^{-23} \text{ kg·m/s}$$

**3. Its kinetic energy, in electron volts.**

For a free electron, $K = p^2 / (2m_e)$:

$$K = \frac{(2.53 \times 10^{-23})^2}{2 \times (9.11 \times 10^{-31})} \approx 3.51 \times 10^{-16} \text{ J}$$

Converting to eV (using $1 \text{ J} = 6.242 \times 10^{18}$ eV):

$$K \approx 2200 \text{ eV}$$
</Example>

<Section letter="B" title="Normalisation Conditions" />

Above, we established that the absolute square of the wave function $|\psi(x)|^2$ gives us the probability of finding the particle at a specific spot. Because the particle definitely exists *somewhere*, if you add up the probabilities for every single possible location in the universe — from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ — the total must equal exactly **1** (or 100%).

Mathematically, adding up everything means taking an integral. This gives us the master equation for normalization:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} |\psi(x)|^2 \, dx = 1$$

To see the math in action, let's imagine a particle trapped in a box of width $L$ (the famous **particle in a box**, which we'll meet next).

Its wave function starts as a standard sine wave:

$$\psi(x) = A \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$

where $A$ is just an unknown constant. To normalize this wave (i.e. make sure its total probability = 1):

**Step 1.** Plug the equation into the normalization integral:

$$\int_0^L \left(A \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\right)^2 dx = 1$$

(We use bounds $0$ to $L$ because the particle is bounded by the box.)

**Step 2.** $A$ is a constant, so square it and pull it outside the integral:

$$A^2 \int_0^L \sin^2\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) dx = 1$$

**Step 3.** Integrating $\sin^2$ directly is hard. Use the trig identity $\sin^2(\theta) = \frac{1 - \cos(2\theta)}{2}$:

$$A^2 \int_0^L \frac{1 - \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right)}{2} dx = 1$$

**Step 4.** Pull out the $\frac{1}{2}$ and split the integral:

$$\frac{A^2}{2} \left[\int_0^L 1 \, dx - \int_0^L \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right) dx\right] = 1$$

The first integral is just $L$. The second integral is the average of a cosine wave over a whole number of full wavelengths — the positive humps cancel the negative dips exactly, so it equals $0$.

$$\frac{A^2}{2}[L - 0] = 1$$

**Step 5.** Solve for $A$:

$$A^2 = \frac{2}{L} \quad \Rightarrow \quad A = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}$$

**Step 6.** Plug it back in:

<KeyResult>
$$\psi(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$
The normalized wave function for a particle in a box.
</KeyResult>

<Section letter="C" title="Expectation Value ⟨x⟩" />

The expectation value answers a specific question: *if I were to measure the position of this exact particle thousands of times, what would the average result be?*

To find this mathematically, we use another integral. You take the wave function, multiply it by its complex conjugate ($\psi^*$), but **sandwich an $x$ right in the middle**:

$$\langle x \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \psi^* \, x \, \psi \, dx$$

Let's apply this to the normalized wave function we just derived:

$$\psi(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)$$

**Step 1.** Because this wave function doesn't have imaginary numbers, its complex conjugate is exactly the same as the original. With bounds $0$ to $L$:

$$\langle x \rangle = \int_0^L \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) \cdot x \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) dx$$

**Step 2.** Simplify — multiply the two $\sqrt{2/L}$ terms to get $2/L$, pull it outside, combine the sines into $\sin^2$:

$$\langle x \rangle = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L x \sin^2\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right) dx$$

**Step 3.** Use the same trig identity as before:

$$\langle x \rangle = \frac{2}{L} \int_0^L x \cdot \frac{1 - \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right)}{2} dx = \frac{1}{L} \int_0^L x \left(1 - \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right)\right) dx$$

**Step 4.** Distribute $x$ and split into two integrals:

$$\langle x \rangle = \frac{1}{L} \left[\int_0^L x \, dx - \int_0^L x \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{L}\right) dx\right]$$

**Step 5.** The first integral is just $\int_0^L x \, dx = L^2 / 2$.

**Step 6.** The second integral *looks* like it needs integration by parts, but there's a physics shortcut: it's the average of $x \cos(\ldots)$ over full wavelengths of cosine. By symmetry, this evaluates to zero.

**Step 7.** Plug both back in:

$$\langle x \rangle = \frac{1}{L} \left[\frac{L^2}{2} - 0\right] = \frac{L^2}{2L}$$

<KeyResult>
$$\langle x \rangle = \frac{L}{2}$$
The average position is dead center. 💡🎯
</KeyResult>

The math actually proves what common sense suggests — and explains why we were using $L/2$ as $\Delta x$ when finding the uncertainty in momentum via the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.
